# VIGIL-2026-RESEARCH-0006

# VIGIL-2026-RESEARCH-0006 — Conflict-condition threat signals, defensive interception and offensive-authority renewal

## Abstract

This reanalysis resets the evidentiary posture of RESEARCH-0006.

The central finding is not that existing military law, doctrine or policy has solved the authority-separation problem. Those materials establish what states, commanders, operators and system designers are expected or required to do. They do not establish what a deployed system actually does.

The public record supports five narrower conclusions.

First, international humanitarian law and military policy distinguish legal authority, target verification, precautions, command responsibility, defensive action and weapons employment. These are normative and doctrinal findings.

Second, public military-AI policies increasingly require human judgement, training, testing, auditability, lifecycle assurance and safeguards. Again, these are requirements and declarations, not proof of deployment compliance.

Third, operational evidence demonstrates that the existence of a human approval or supervisory step is not itself evidence of meaningful human review. The U.S. Patriot fratricide experience is a particularly important military example: Army human-factors work identified automation misuse, automation bias, vigilance problems, training limitations and excessive trust in the automated system despite human operators remaining in the control structure. In a different context, the IDF's own World Central Kitchen investigation concluded that a lethal strike followed mistaken identification, decision errors and serious violation of commands and standard operating procedures. These examples do not prove the specific RESEARCH-0006 authority-coupling hypothesis. They do prove that formal procedures and human presence cannot be assumed to perform their intended assurance function in deployment.

Fourth, evidence concerning Habsora/Gospel and Lavender remains materially contested. The IDF states that the systems assist analysts, cannot autonomously identify targets for attack, cannot be the sole basis for target identification and sit within a broader human targeting process. Investigative reporting based on Israeli intelligence sources alleges substantially more automated target generation and, for some Lavender-related review, extremely compressed human examination described by sources as approximately twenty seconds per target and as rubber-stamping. Public evidence does not provide independent access to the relevant logs, interfaces, rejection rates, underlying intelligence, authorization records or deployed technical configuration. The existence of a formal human step is therefore documented or institutionally asserted; meaningful independent human review is not established.

Fifth, AI-safety research and mature safety-regulatory practice support version-bounded assurance. Fine-tuning and other system changes can degrade previously observed safety behaviour. Regulators such as the FDA require bounded change plans, validation, impact assessment and monitoring for evolving AI-enabled medical devices. Military-AI policy likewise calls for testing and assurance across lifecycles. This supports the governance proposition that a control demonstrated in one version should not silently be inherited by a materially changed successor. It does not establish that a particular military system lost a specific authority-separation control after an update.

No reviewed public evidence establishes a deployed technical mechanism in which detection of a hostile event automatically creates, revives, transfers or propagates offensive execution authority. The review also found no deployment-level evidence sufficient to establish that such propagation is technically impossible or universally excluded in relevant deployed systems.

Accordingly, the "perpetual war token" remains an analytical metaphor, not an observed technology. The broader concerns are better characterised as: **an empirically supported human-assurance hazard; an externally defensible authority-separation architectural hazard; and a version-bounded-assurance requirement whose military implementation remains publicly under-verified.**

---

## Research question

The primary question is:

> Can detection of a hostile event, continuation of a conflict condition, standing defensive authority, or an immediate-defence exception improperly become sufficient — through law, doctrine, human process, software architecture, permission state or system integration — to create, preserve, extend, revive, transfer or inherit authority for offensive force without an independently accountable authorisation step?

Subsidiary questions are:

- What form of "authority" is actually at issue at each stage?
- Which distinctions exist in law and doctrine?
- Which controls are merely declared?
- Which controls are demonstrated in deployed systems?
- What evidence exists that human review is substantive rather than nominal?
- What evidence exists that governance-relevant controls persist after system change?
- Where does the public evidence stop?

---

## Scope and methodology

### Research reset and epistemic posture

This record applies a strict rule:

> **A requirement is not an implementation. An implementation is not a deployment. A deployment is not proof of operational effectiveness. Operational effectiveness in one version is not proof of persistence in a successor version.**

The following inferences are prohibited unless separately evidenced:

- policy requires X → deployed system performs X;
- institution states X → X occurred operationally;
- human sign-off exists → meaningful human judgement occurred;
- a safeguard was tested once → the safeguard persists after material system change;
- no public example was found → the mechanism does not exist.

The report therefore distinguishes **absence of evidence** from **evidence of absence**.

---

### Methodology

Sources were classified by both confidence and evidentiary layer.

### Confidence classifications

- **established / directly documented**
- **corroborated**
- **credible but contested**
- **single-source**
- **institutionally asserted**
- **inferred**
- **speculative**
- **unsupported**

### Evidence layers

1. **Law / normative obligation** — what binding law requires.
2. **Doctrine / policy / standard** — what organisations prescribe.
3. **Declared implementation** — what an operator, military, vendor or government says a system does.
4. **Designed architecture** — what design documentation indicates was intended.
5. **Implemented architecture** — what evidence shows was built.
6. **Deployed architecture** — what evidence shows was actually running.
7. **Observed operational behaviour** — what the system and humans demonstrably did.
8. **Assured operational behaviour** — independently verified reliable performance.
9. **Lifecycle persistence** — evidence that a governance property survived material change.

A high-confidence Layer 2 claim remains a Layer 2 claim.

---

### Evidence-layer framework

The evidentiary problem can be represented as:

**Normative requirement**  
↓ separate evidence required  
**Declared or designed control**  
↓ separate evidence required  
**Implemented control**  
↓ separate evidence required  
**Deployed control**  
↓ separate evidence required  
**Operationally effective control**  
↓ separate evidence required  
**Control preserved after change**

The strongest public sources in this review are not evenly distributed across those layers. Law and policy are comparatively visible. Deployed military software architecture, operator interfaces, permission logic and change histories are not.

This asymmetry is itself a material research finding.

## Findings

### Normative requirement versus deployment

The UN Charter preserves the inherent right of individual or collective self-defence in Article 51 when an armed attack occurs. That is a legal rule concerning state use of force; it is not a software permission model.

[Additional Protocol I Article 57](https://ihl-databases.icrc.org/en/ihl-treaties/api-1977/article-57) governs precautions in attack irrespective of whether an operation is described as offensive or defensive. It requires those who plan or decide upon attacks to take feasible steps to verify targets, choose means and methods to reduce civilian harm, assess expected incidental harm, and cancel or suspend attacks where required. The ICRC commentary expressly notes that the precautions apply to attacks whether they are acts of aggression or responses to aggression.

These sources establish that an incoming attack or defensive posture does not erase attack-specific IHL obligations. They do **not** establish what a particular command-and-control system technically permits after a hostile event.

Similarly, the [U.S. DoD's update to Directive 3000.09](https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/3278076/dod-announces-update-to-dod-directive-300009-autonomy-in-weapon-systems/) requires autonomous and semi-autonomous weapon systems to be designed so commanders and operators can exercise appropriate levels of human judgement over the use of force and requires realistic performance, reliability, effectiveness and suitability. The [Political Declaration on Responsible Military Use of AI and Autonomy](https://2021-2025.state.gov/political-declaration-on-responsible-military-use-of-artificial-intelligence-and-autonomy) calls for oversight, operator training, auditable development, testing and assurance across lifecycles, including monitoring so critical safety features are not degraded in self-learning or continuously updating systems.

Those are material normative and policy controls. They cannot be cited as proof that every fielded system conforms.

---

### Authority terminology and authority-layer model

The original expression "offensive reauthorisation" compresses multiple authority layers and should be used cautiously.

### 7.1 Distinct authority layers

**Jus ad bellum / state self-defence authority**  
Concerns whether a state may resort to force under international law.

**Domestic constitutional/statutory authority**  
Concerns internal legal authority to conduct military operations.

**Political or national-command authorization**  
Concerns decisions by competent national authorities.

**Military command authority**  
Concerns command responsibility, mission authorization and tasking.

**Rules of engagement / delegated engagement authority**  
Operational rules defining when and how force may be used, including standing or pre-delegated permissions.

**Unit self-defence / immediate defensive engagement**  
May permit rapid response to hostile acts or hostile intent under applicable rules.

**Target-development and validation authority**  
Concerns identification of an object or person as a lawful military objective.

**Target approval / dynamic targeting authority**  
Concerns authorization to prosecute a target within a mission and operational context.

**Weapons-release authority**  
Concerns the final authority to employ a weapon.

**Technical execution permission**  
Concerns what software, hardware, interfaces and permission states actually allow a system or operator to execute.

These layers can overlap operationally, but they are not synonyms.

### 7.2 Terminology conclusion

"Offensive reauthorisation" is best treated as a **governance abstraction** for renewal or propagation across one or more operational authority layers. It should not be presented as a settled legal term.

---

### Legal framework

### 8.1 Self-defence

Article 51 of the UN Charter recognises an inherent right of self-defence if an armed attack occurs. The scope, necessity, proportionality and temporal dimensions of self-defence are matters of international law and state practice beyond the technical scope of this record.

For VIGIL purposes, the important point is narrower: a legal basis for self-defence is not equivalent to a machine-readable weapons-release token.

### 8.2 Conduct of hostilities

IHL requires distinction, proportionality and precautions in attack. Article 57 of Additional Protocol I requires feasible target verification and ongoing control, including cancellation or suspension when an attack no longer satisfies applicable conditions.

The legal framework therefore supports **continued human/legal responsibility across the attack process**. It does not specify how a particular military AI system must encode that responsibility.

### 8.3 Human responsibility

The ICRC consistently argues that legal obligations governing attacks are addressed to humans and that responsibility cannot be transferred to software. Its 2026 military-AI FAQ separately identifies AI decision-support systems as presenting risks of unreliability, unpredictability and human over-reliance.

This is an important normative and analytical finding, not proof that deployed systems preserve meaningful human judgement.

---

### Military doctrine and policy

The reviewed U.S., UK and multilateral policy sources show convergence around several ideas:

- human responsibility should remain identifiable;
- personnel must understand system capabilities and limitations;
- automation bias is a recognised risk;
- systems should be tested and assured;
- safeguards should exist for unintended behaviour;
- governance should apply across the lifecycle;
- material changes may require renewed assurance.

The Political Declaration is unusually relevant because it expressly states that military AI should be subject to rigorous testing and assurance across entire lifecycles and that self-learning or continuously updating systems should be monitored to ensure critical safety features are not degraded.

The UK Defence AI Strategy and its "Ambitious, Safe, Responsible" framework likewise describe lifecycle governance, auditable human responsibility, testing, recertification of operators and through-life support.

These sources provide strong support for the **principle** of lifecycle assurance.

They do not establish implementation quality in any particular deployed system.

---

### Defensive interception

Iron Dome is publicly described as a radar-supported defensive interception system that detects rocket launches, predicts trajectories and selectively engages threats assessed as heading toward populated areas.

This supports a material functional distinction between:

- threat detection;
- trajectory assessment;
- bounded defensive interception.

It does not establish:

- the software architecture of offensive targeting systems;
- whether defensive and offensive systems share permission state;
- whether a defensive trigger can propagate into another system;
- whether authority state is technically segregated.

The defensive function is therefore **documented**; the wider authority architecture remains **unverified**.

---

### Offensive targeting and weapons-release authority

The [IDF's June 2024 public description of Habsora and Lavender](https://www.idf.il/en/mini-sites/idf-press-releases-israel-at-war/june-24-pr/the-idfs-use-of-data-technologies-in-intelligence-processing-published-june-18-2024/) states that:

- the tools help analysts process and cross-reference intelligence;
- target identification must be performed by a human analyst;
- an authorized intelligence officer must approve identified targets;
- operational commanders decide whether to attack;
- technological tools cannot be the sole basis for target identification.

This is a detailed **declared implementation** and policy description.

It is not independent evidence that every deployed workflow complied with those requirements.

That distinction is central because independent reporting alleges a materially different operational picture in at least some periods of the war.

---

### Threat-event triggering and pre-delegated authority

Military operations can legitimately contain event-triggered or pre-delegated authority. Examples include rapid defensive engagement against incoming threats and standing rules that allow immediate action under defined conditions.

Accordingly, the research question cannot be framed as "all force after a hostile event requires a wholly new legal authorization." That would overstate the legal and operational position.

The relevant governance boundary is narrower:

> Does the hostile event merely satisfy a pre-existing defensive rule, trigger reassessment, or supply evidence — or does it technically or institutionally restore a broader permission state that should have required separate accountable judgment?

Public sources reviewed for RESEARCH-0006 do not expose sufficient command or technical state information to answer that question for a named AI-enabled offensive system.

---

### AI and automation in military decision pathways

Military AI may operate in several distinct roles:

- sensor processing;
- classification;
- data fusion;
- intelligence prioritisation;
- pattern recognition;
- indications and warnings;
- target nomination or recommendation;
- battle management;
- defensive interception;
- autonomous engagement;
- command-and-control support.

These functions must not be collapsed.

The U.S. Army War College's Raven Sentry case is useful because it describes an AI indications-and-warnings system used in Afghanistan and explicitly warns operators about automation bias. Its creators reportedly treated AI output as one input requiring corroboration from traditional intelligence disciplines.

This is evidence that military practitioners recognise a difference between **AI-generated indication** and **authority to act**.

It is not evidence that every deployed military AI workflow maintains that separation.

---

### Meaningful human review versus nominal human presence

### 14.1 Review-quality test

A meaningful human review requires more than a person appearing in a workflow.

Relevant questions include:

- Did the reviewer receive underlying evidence or only a recommendation?
- Could the reviewer inspect the recommendation's basis?
- Was sufficient time available?
- Was workload compatible with independent judgement?
- Could contradictory information reach the reviewer?
- Did the reviewer possess practical authority to reject or stop?
- Was rejection institutionally tolerated?
- Is there evidence of actual correction, rejection or escalation?
- Was the reviewer trained to recognise automation failure?
- Was review quality independently tested?

Where these facts are unknown, the appropriate finding is:

> **Formal human involvement may be documented; substantive review quality remains unestablished.**

### 14.2 Military operational evidence: Patriot

The Patriot experience provides direct evidence that nominal human control can fail to deliver substantive assurance.

During Operation Iraqi Freedom in 2003, Patriot units were involved in two fratricide incidents. Subsequent U.S. Army human-factors analysis, later summarised by Army Research Laboratory researcher John K. Hawley, identified problems including undisciplined automation, automation misuse, vigilance limitations, training weaknesses and excessive trust in automated recommendations.

Importantly, humans remained within the engagement structure.

The governance failure was not "no human existed." The problem was that the human-machine system did not reliably produce the independent judgement attributed to the human role.

Patriot therefore materially supports the generic mechanism captured internally by FM-0059.

It does **not** demonstrate the specific defensive-to-offensive authority propagation hypothesised in FM-0060 or FM-0061.

---

### Human-factors evidence and assurance failure

The broader human-factors literature supports the Patriot lesson.

Research on automation reliance has repeatedly identified both misuse — excessive reliance on automated advice — and disuse — inappropriate rejection of automation. A 2001 combat-identification study examined operator reliance on automated decision aids in a military context. More recent experimental work in national-security decision contexts similarly finds conditions under which automation bias can occur.

The ICRC's 2026 military-AI guidance explicitly warns that AI decision-support speed and scale can encourage over-reliance and recommends training against automation bias, meaningful human engagement, the ability to challenge outputs, rigorous testing and after-action review.

These findings support a general proposition:

> **Human approval is an assurance mechanism only when the surrounding information, interface, training, time, workload, authority and organisational conditions permit genuine judgement.**

### Israel–Gaza case study

### 16.1 Defensive system

Iron Dome provides the clearest public example of a bounded defensive interception role in the reviewed theatre. Its existence does not establish integration with offensive targeting authority.

### 16.2 Resumption of hostilities after the November 2023 truce

Reuters and AP reported renewed rocket fire and resumed Israeli strikes after the temporary truce collapsed on 1 December 2023.

This establishes temporal sequence.

It does not establish:

- that a rocket event automatically restored offensive authority;
- that any AI system performed the authorization transition;
- that offensive authority had previously expired;
- that a software token or permission state was involved.

### 16.3 Habsora/Gospel and Lavender: official position

The IDF confirms use of Habsora and Lavender-related data technologies but states that they support human intelligence analysis and do not autonomously identify targets for attack.

The IDF also states that target identification is distinct from the command decision to attack.

This is significant counter-evidence to claims of fully autonomous target selection.

Its evidentiary layer is principally **declared implementation**, supported by the institution's description of its own procedures.

### 16.4 Lavender: investigative reporting

Investigative reporting by +972 Magazine/Local Call, subsequently reported by the Guardian and Reuters, cited Israeli intelligence sources alleging that Lavender was used at scale to identify suspected militants and that human review of some outputs was highly compressed.

The widely repeated "approximately twenty seconds" claim originates from source testimony about parts of that workflow. It should not be generalised to every target, every reviewer or every period.

The claim is therefore classified:

- **credible but contested**;
- based materially on confidential-source testimony;
- relevant to **observed/reported operational behaviour**;
- not independently verified through logs or direct technical access.

The proper conclusion is not that meaningful review definitely failed in every Lavender case.

It is:

> **Public evidence does not establish that the formal human review described by the IDF was consistently substantive, independent and adequately informed in deployed operations. Credible reporting raises a material contrary possibility that remains unresolved.**

### 16.5 Human approval is not dispositive

The official and investigative accounts can both contain partial truths.

A human may have formally approved a target while:

- relying heavily on algorithmically prioritised information;
- having limited review time;
- reviewing a large throughput of recommendations;
- lacking practical opportunity to reconstruct the underlying intelligence case.

Accordingly, "a human approved" does not resolve the assurance question.

### 16.6 World Central Kitchen: procedure versus deployment

The IDF's own investigation into the 1 April 2024 World Central Kitchen strike is important for methodology.

The inquiry concluded that seven aid workers were killed after mistaken identification, errors in decision-making and strikes carried out in serious violation of commands and standard operating procedures.

This incident is not evidence about Lavender or automated authority propagation.

It is, however, direct operational evidence against the inference:

> **"A procedure exists, therefore the procedure was followed."**

The incident demonstrates that even a formally governed lethal decision process can depart from its own commands and SOPs in deployment.

That finding materially strengthens the requirement to distinguish policy from observed practice.

### 16.7 Commercial AI and cloud infrastructure

AP reporting in 2025 documented substantial use of U.S.-provided commercial AI and cloud infrastructure in Israeli military operations. Later reporting described continuing expansion of military data and AI infrastructure.

These sources establish material technology dependence and integration.

They do not establish shared legal authority, shared permission states or automatic weapons-release authority.

---

### Comparative deployment evidence

### 17.1 Patriot

**Evidence layer:** observed operational behaviour + post-incident human-factors assessment.

**What it establishes:** human operators can remain formally in or on the loop while automation bias, workload, design and training impair the assurance role.

**What it does not establish:** offensive-authority propagation.

### 17.2 Raven Sentry

**Evidence layer:** official military case description / declared and reported operational use.

**What it establishes:** deployed AI indications-and-warnings can be treated as one intelligence input requiring corroboration; military practitioners explicitly recognise automation-bias risk.

**What it does not establish:** universal military practice.

### 17.3 World Central Kitchen strike

**Evidence layer:** official operational investigation.

**What it establishes:** lethal decisions can violate existing commands and SOPs because of misidentification and decision failure.

**What it does not establish:** AI caused the incident.

These comparative examples are more probative for the policy-to-deployment gap than additional high-level policy statements.

---

### Defensive–offensive technical coupling

Public evidence demonstrates that modern military operations can share:

- sensors;
- data;
- cloud infrastructure;
- common operational pictures;
- intelligence platforms;
- battle-management software;
- communications networks.

Shared infrastructure is not the same as shared authority.

A system may share sensor data while maintaining separate engagement permissions.

Conversely, separate doctrinal categories do not prove that technical permission states are segregated.

For the Israel-Gaza systems reviewed, public evidence is insufficient to establish:

- a common defensive/offensive permission store;
- a shared authority token;
- a technical rule propagating a defensive trigger into offensive execution;
- a single kill switch controlling both defensive and offensive functions.

FM-0060 therefore remains an **architectural hazard with deployed occurrence unverified**.

---

### Deployment evidence for authority separation

The strongest positive evidence for separation is currently at the policy and declared-workflow levels.

Examples include:

- IHL obligations that attach to each attack;
- DoD policy requiring human judgement;
- IDF claims that intelligence identification and operational attack decisions are distinct;
- military policy requiring training, testing and command accountability.

These are not deployment-level proof.

The strongest deployment-level evidence found in this review is indirect:

- Raven Sentry was reportedly used as an input requiring corroboration rather than as independent authority;
- Patriot demonstrates the converse risk that a nominal human decision structure can still fail;
- the WCK inquiry demonstrates that formal procedures can be breached.

The public record therefore supports neither of the following categorical claims:

> "deployed military systems routinely collapse defensive and offensive authority"

nor

> "existing military controls demonstrably prevent such collapse."

The correct status is **under-verified at deployment level**.

---

### Authority state, persistence, suspension and revalidation

Military authority is not a single state variable.

Some authority may be:

- standing;
- mission-bounded;
- target-specific;
- geographically bounded;
- temporally bounded;
- conditionally activated;
- suspended;
- revoked;
- delegated.

A hostile event may legitimately activate a pre-existing defensive rule or prompt reassessment.

The governance risk arises where a system silently converts one class of authority into another without the decision or conditions required for the second class.

No public evidence reviewed establishes that such a conversion occurred automatically in the relevant Israel-Gaza AI systems.

No reviewed source provides enough technical permission-state information to affirmatively exclude it.

---

### Model/system updates and governance-control regression

### 21.1 Empirical AI evidence

Research on aligned language models demonstrates that safety-relevant behaviour can change after fine-tuning.

Qi et al. showed that both adversarial and benign fine-tuning can degrade safety alignment in previously aligned models. Subsequent work has continued to study alignment degradation and methods to preserve safety through model modification.

The relevance to military governance is limited but important:

- it does **not** prove military authority controls are implemented in model weights;
- it does **not** prove that a military update caused an authority leak;
- it does demonstrate that previously observed behavioural safeguards cannot automatically be assumed to survive model modification.

### 21.2 System-level change

Governance behaviour can depend on more than model weights. It may be distributed across:

- system prompts;
- classifiers;
- orchestration;
- retrieval;
- tool permissions;
- software logic;
- operator interfaces;
- command workflows;
- data sources;
- access control;
- human training.

A governance property is therefore a property of a **versioned sociotechnical system**, not merely of a policy document.

### 21.3 Regulatory evidence

The FDA's approach to AI-enabled device software is instructive because it requires bounded change plans, validation methods, impact assessment, monitoring and evidence of continued safety and effectiveness.

This is cross-domain evidence, not military law.

It supports the general assurance principle that material AI changes require controlled validation rather than assumption of inherited safety.

---

### Version-bounded assurance

The following proposition is externally defensible:

> **Assurance should be bounded to the model, configuration, architecture, operational context and version actually evaluated unless preservation or equivalence is demonstrated.**

This is supported by:

- empirical evidence of safety regression after fine-tuning;
- AI lifecycle risk-management practice;
- FDA change-control and lifecycle regulation;
- military policy requiring lifecycle testing and assurance;
- DoD software-acquisition practice recognising iterative delivery and ongoing testing.

For RESEARCH-0006, the governance property of interest is:

> **A defensive threat signal or defensive engagement permission cannot improperly propagate into offensive execution permission.**

If that property were demonstrated in version A, reasonable evidence for version B would include, depending on system design:

- documented change impact assessment;
- traceable configuration/version identity;
- regression testing of the non-propagation property;
- permission-state tests;
- scenario testing under realistic operational conditions;
- operator-interface and human-factors retesting where relevant;
- audit logs demonstrating expected transitions;
- revalidation after changes to models, orchestration, rules, permissions or command interfaces.

This is a governance and assurance conclusion, not evidence that a named military system currently implements these tests.

---

### Independent challenge and ground-truth channels

Humanitarian organisations, legal advisers, neutral intermediaries, civilian-harm monitoring and independent investigations can provide evidence that challenges military data pipelines.

Their mere existence does not establish incorporation.

Four separate questions must be asked:

1. Can contradictory evidence reach the decision process?
2. Is anyone required to examine it?
3. Can that person halt or revise the decision?
4. Is there evidence this happens operationally?

UNICEF and ICRC reporting demonstrate independent humanitarian and intermediary evidence channels.

The WCK incident demonstrates why the transition from "information exists" to "information effectively changes a lethal decision" cannot be assumed.

## Counter-evidence and alternative explanations

The research actively sought evidence that would reduce concern.

### Evidence that narrows the concern

- Law and military policy clearly preserve human responsibility.
- DoD Directive 3000.09 expressly requires appropriate human judgement and realistic testing.
- The IDF publicly describes Habsora/Lavender as decision-support tools rather than autonomous target selectors.
- Raven Sentry provides an example of AI output being treated as an input requiring corroboration.
- The Political Declaration explicitly recognises automation bias and lifecycle degradation as risks requiring controls.

These findings show that the VIGIL concern is not novel in the sense that external governance has ignored human responsibility or lifecycle assurance.

### Evidence that does not falsify the deployment hazard

None of the above, by itself, establishes:

- technical separation of defensive/offensive permission states;
- meaningful review in every deployed target workflow;
- non-propagation after model/software changes;
- operational compliance with written procedures.

### Strongest falsifying evidence not found

The review did not locate independently accessible:

- deployed permission-state architecture proving defensive/offensive non-propagation;
- audit evidence demonstrating that a named military AI targeting system consistently prevents stale authority inheritance;
- longitudinal assurance evidence showing the relevant control survived material system updates.

This is an absence of public evidence, not evidence that those controls do not exist.

---

### Absence of evidence versus evidence of absence

### Absence of evidence

The review found no public documentation establishing that:

- an incoming defensive threat automatically reactivated expired offensive technical permission;
- a "perpetual war token" or functional equivalent existed;
- Iron Dome shared an authority-control plane with Habsora, Lavender or another offensive targeting system;
- a named deployed military AI system lost an authority-separation safeguard after model update.

### Evidence of absence

The review found limited evidence of absence only within bounded declared or tested contexts.

For example, the IDF states that its data-analysis tools do not themselves generate attack orders and cannot be the sole basis for identifying targets. That is evidence of the institution's declared architecture and procedures. It is not independent technical verification of all deployed configurations and periods.

No broad deployment-level evidence of absence was identified for the core coupling hypothesis.

### External factual findings

The following are the strongest externally grounded findings.

1. IHL imposes attack-specific verification and precaution obligations; defensive status does not remove those obligations.
2. Military AI policy recognises the need for human judgement, testing, training, auditability and lifecycle controls.
3. Deployed automated military systems can produce human-assurance failure even when humans formally remain in the control architecture, as demonstrated by the Patriot experience.
4. Formal military commands and SOPs can fail in actual lethal operations, as the IDF's WCK inquiry itself establishes.
5. The IDF acknowledges operational use of Habsora/Lavender-related data technologies and describes a human targeting process.
6. Independent reporting raises credible but contested concerns about scale, automation pressure and compressed review in Lavender-related targeting.
7. Commercial AI and cloud infrastructure became materially integrated into Israeli military operations.
8. Public evidence does not expose the technical permission-state architecture necessary to test defensive-to-offensive authority propagation directly.
9. AI safety behaviour can regress after fine-tuning and other system changes.
10. Mature AI governance regimes increasingly require lifecycle monitoring and revalidation rather than assuming unchanged safety.

---

### Analytical synthesis

The original RESEARCH-0006 question remains useful, but its evidentiary basis must be reframed.

The strongest evidence does **not** support a claim that a deployed military system possesses a known "perpetual war token" or automatically converts hostile-event detection into renewed offensive authority.

The stronger and more defensible finding is architectural:

> Military governance increasingly depends on AI and automated systems that can compress sensing, classification, prioritisation and decision tempo. Law and policy preserve human responsibility, but operational history demonstrates that nominal human involvement and written procedures can fail to deliver meaningful assurance. Because the relevant deployed technical architectures and permission states are rarely public, governance should not infer implementation from doctrine or infer continuing control from prior-version assurance.

This supports a control model requiring:

- explicit separation of threat evidence from authority state;
- non-propagation between bounded defensive permissions and broader offensive permissions;
- substantive rather than ceremonial human review where human judgement is relied upon;
- traceable version/configuration identity;
- revalidation after material change.

These are prudent controls justified by cross-domain and military assurance evidence even where the specific hypothesised deployment failure remains unverified.

## Limitations

1. Classified rules of engagement and command authorizations are unavailable.
2. Deployed source code, permission-state architecture and logs are unavailable.
3. Lavender reporting depends substantially on confidential Israeli intelligence sources and remains contested.
4. The IDF's public account is detailed but institutionally self-reported.
5. Patriot is a highly automated defensive system but not a contemporary machine-learning targeting model.
6. WCK demonstrates procedural and human decision failure, not AI causation.
7. LLM fine-tuning studies demonstrate general governance-property regression, not military authority regression.
8. FDA and NIST lifecycle practices are cross-domain analogues, not military legal requirements.
9. No public audit was found that longitudinally verifies defensive/offensive permission separation across versions of a named deployed military AI system.

## Governance implications

### Implications for RESEARCH-0006 internal constructs

The internal constructs should be revised as follows.

### "Threat signal is evidence, not authority"

**Retain, but qualify.**

A hostile event can legitimately satisfy a pre-existing defensive rule or contribute to an authorization decision. The principle should therefore mean:

> A threat signal does not, by itself and outside a valid standing or delegated rule, establish broader authority for unrelated or expanded offensive force.

### "Defensive exception is non-portable"

**Retain as a governance safeguard.**

Immediate defensive engagement may be valid and rapid. Its existence should not silently broaden another system's target or execution permissions.

### "Offensive authority must expire and be renewed"

**Narrow terminology.**

Not every lawful military authority expires after each hostile event or pause. Different layers of authority have different persistence rules. The better requirement is that **time-, target-, mission-, geographic- or condition-bounded authorities must not be silently treated as continuing after their governing conditions lapse.**

### "Independent offensive reauthorisation"

**Rename or define more precisely.**

Use "independently accountable authorization/revalidation where the relevant authority has expired, been suspended, materially changed, or falls outside a standing delegation."

### "Human approval"

**Strengthen.**

Where human judgement is the assurance mechanism, require evidence that the human can actually inspect, understand, challenge, reject and stop.

### Lifecycle control

**Add explicitly.**

The non-propagation property must be version-bounded and revalidated after material model, software, orchestration, permission, interface or workflow change.

---

### Implications for broader VIGIL authority-separation model

### FM-0059 — Human-in-the-loop assurance failure

External evidence strongly supports the generic mechanism.

Patriot provides a military operational example of automation reliance degrading effective human control. WCK provides a separate operational example showing that formal procedures and human approval structures can fail in lethal decision-making.

FM-0059 should not depend on the Lavender allegations for validity.

### FM-0060 — Defensive–offensive autonomy scope compression and control coupling

Recommended classification:

> **Externally defensible architectural hazard; deployed occurrence unverified.**

The risk is conceptually and technically plausible. Public evidence does not establish a named deployed shared-control failure.

### FM-0061 — Conflict-event authority reactivation without independent offensive reauthorisation

Recommended classification:

> **Governance hazard; automatic deployed occurrence unverified; deployment-level exclusion not established.**

Legitimate pre-delegated and continuing authorities exist, so the failure mode must not treat every renewed operation as requiring wholly fresh legal authorization. It should focus on silent revival, extension or inheritance **outside the conditions of valid standing authority**.

### PROP-0032

The proposal remains defensible if it is framed as a precautionary authority-separation and assurance architecture rather than as a repair for a proven deployed "perpetual war token."

It should explicitly include:

- evidence-state versus authority-state separation;
- defensive/offensive permission non-propagation;
- substantive human assurance requirements;
- version/configuration-bound assurance;
- revalidation after material system change.

### Assessment of the "perpetual war token" abstraction

### Literal deployed mechanism

**Unsupported.**

No evidence identifies a literal token, flag or software object with this function.

### Functionally equivalent automatic authority revival

**Plausible but unverified deployment hazard.**

The public technical evidence is insufficient to establish or exclude it in relevant systems.

### Broader governance concern

**Empirically supported.**

Operational evidence shows that:

- automation can compress human judgement;
- formal human presence can become ineffective;
- written procedures can fail in deployment;
- AI safety properties can change after updates;
- high-level policy does not establish technical implementation.

Accordingly, the metaphor should remain only if it is clearly labelled as an analytical shorthand for **unbounded persistence or propagation of force authority**, not as an observed technology.

A less provocative research term would be:

> **conflict-condition authority persistence or propagation hazard**

## Conclusion

The reanalysis does not validate the original "perpetual war token" as a deployed mechanism.

It does validate the reason the governance question matters.

Law and military policy contain meaningful distinctions between self-defence, attack-specific legal obligations, target verification, human judgement and command responsibility. Those distinctions are real, but they are **normative**.

Deployment evidence is materially thinner.

The strongest operational evidence demonstrates that humans and procedures can fail to perform the assurance function attributed to them. Patriot shows that a human can remain nominally in control while automation bias, training, interface design and organisational culture undermine independent judgement. The IDF's own WCK inquiry shows that written commands and SOPs can be breached during lethal operations. The Lavender evidence remains contested, but credible reporting is sufficient to prevent a conclusion that formal human sign-off establishes meaningful review.

The lifecycle evidence adds a second critical boundary: governance properties cannot simply be inherited across material system change. AI research demonstrates that safety-relevant behaviour can regress after fine-tuning; AI regulation and military policy increasingly require lifecycle validation and monitoring.

The most defensible VIGIL position is therefore:

> **Threat detection, bounded defensive engagement, targeting authority and offensive execution permission should be treated as separately governed states. Human review should be evaluated by its substantive assurance function rather than nominal presence. Assurance of these properties should be version- and configuration-bounded and revalidated after material change.**

This is a defensible governance architecture even though the review did not establish a real-world automated defensive-to-offensive authority propagation incident.

## Final decision questions

### 1. What does law require regarding defensive and offensive force?

International law distinguishes the right to self-defence from the conduct of attacks. IHL precautions, distinction and proportionality continue to apply to attacks regardless of a party's defensive characterization. The law does not provide a software permission architecture.

### 2. What does military doctrine or policy require?

Reviewed U.S., UK and multilateral policies require human responsibility, appropriate judgement, training, testing, auditability, safeguards and lifecycle assurance.

### 3. What evidence establishes those requirements are implemented in deployed AI-enabled systems?

Only partial and system-specific evidence. Public material rarely exposes implementation, permission states, operator interfaces or independent audits. Policy itself does not establish deployment.

### 4. What relevant deployment questions remain unverified?

Technical authority state, permission propagation, review interfaces, override/rejection rates, version-specific controls, and post-update revalidation remain substantially unverified.

### 5. Is meaningful human review demonstrated in relevant deployed systems?

Not generally. Formal human involvement is documented or asserted in several systems. In Lavender-related reporting, the substantive quality of review remains contested and unverified.

### 6. What evidence exists that nominal human oversight can fail?

Strong evidence exists. Patriot is a military operational example. Broader human-factors research independently supports automation-bias and over-reliance mechanisms.

### 7. Is there documented evidence of defensive authority technically propagating into offensive authority?

No public example was identified.

### 8. Is there affirmative evidence excluding such propagation in identified deployed systems?

Not at a sufficiently independent technical level for the systems central to this record.

### 9. Is there documented evidence of a hostile event automatically reviving expired or suspended offensive technical permission?

No public example was identified.

### 10. What legitimate forms of event-triggered or pre-delegated authority exist?

Immediate defensive engagement, standing ROE and delegated authorities may legitimately be condition-triggered. Their scope is legally and operationally context-dependent.

### 11. Is defensive/offensive separation demonstrated at policy, technical architecture, deployment, or some combination?

Strongly at normative/policy level; partially at declared workflow level; insufficiently at independently verified deployed technical architecture level.

### 12. Does "offensive reauthorisation" accurately represent the relevant processes?

Only as a defined governance abstraction. External practice contains multiple distinct authority layers, some persistent and some requiring renewed assessment or approval.

### 13. Can assurance be inherited across model/system versions without renewed evidence?

Not safely as a general rule. Material changes require evidence of preservation or renewed validation.

### 14. What evidence exists that AI/system updates can alter governance-relevant behaviour?

Experimental LLM research demonstrates safety degradation after fine-tuning. Regulatory and policy frameworks independently treat change as requiring lifecycle control and monitoring.

### 15. How should the internal VIGIL concerns be classified?

- FM-0059: empirically supported generic assurance failure.
- FM-0060: documented/externally defensible architectural hazard; deployed occurrence unverified.
- FM-0061: externally defensible governance hazard; automatic deployed occurrence unverified.
- "Perpetual war token": unsupported as literal mechanism; plausible metaphor for a broader persistence/propagation hazard.

### 16. Does the current VIGIL authority-separation model remain externally defensible?

Yes, if framed as precautionary governance architecture rather than a factual claim that a particular deployed system exhibits the hypothesised mechanism.

### 17. What should change in RESEARCH-0006 or related VIGIL analysis?

- replace policy→deployment inference with the evidence-layer model;
- incorporate FM-0059 and external human-assurance evidence;
- add version-bounded assurance;
- narrow "offensive reauthorisation";
- classify FM-0060/0061 as hazards unless deployment evidence emerges;
- treat institutional statements as declared implementation unless independently verified;
- preserve absence-of-evidence language;
- require substantive human review rather than mere human presence.

### Source-use warning

The bibliography intentionally contains sources at different evidentiary layers.

An authoritative legal or policy source may strongly establish a **requirement** while providing no evidence of **deployment**.

An investigative report may provide important operational evidence while remaining contested.

An institutional source may accurately describe an organisation's stated architecture while remaining non-independent.

No source should be cited outside the evidentiary role identified above.
