# SOCI reporting and broader AI reporting policy

Date: 18 October 2026. Classification: REVIEW. AI-authored; no human approval claimed. Research and concrete proposals complete; canonical implementation awaits the SOCI-specific decision required by handoff section 3.

SOCI is an existing binding reporting framework for responsible entities of covered critical infrastructure assets, subject to Part 2B application and all statutory/rule conditions. It is not a general duty imposed on every AI developer. The operative Act is [C2026C00213](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2026C00213/latest/text); sections 30BB–30BF must be read with sections 8G, 9, 12L–12N, 13–14 and the Application and Definitions Rules. The companion coverage report sets out these dependencies and the separate 12/72-hour initial and 84/48-hour oral-follow-up clocks.

An AI-mediated unauthorised access, modification or disruption could satisfy section 12M and the relevant impact conditions when it concerns a covered asset and governed entity. AI involvement alone supplies neither asset coverage nor the impact/knowledge trigger. Critical reporting requires actual significant availability impact; other reporting includes specified imminent/likely cases. A model error without the defined cyber incident and covered asset/actor conditions is outside demonstrated SOCI reporting coverage. This is a limit of the demonstrated framework, not a finding that no other law applies.

Other Australian notification regimes have distinct predicates. [Privacy Act 1988](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A03712/latest/text), Part IIIC, sections 26WK–26WL, concerns eligible data breach statements to the Commissioner and notification of affected individuals under its own conditions. [Cyber Security Act 2024](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2024A00098/latest/text), Part 3, sections 26–27, concerns qualifying ransomware/cyber-extortion payments by reporting business entities: the 72-hour clock concerns payment or awareness of payment made, rather than SOCI incident-awareness timing. Neither is interchangeable with SOCI or a public disclosure. These texts were retrieved for this bounded distinction; no new source admission, threshold-rule opinion or occurrence-specific applicability finding is proposed for either regime.

INC-003 is US-centred. No Australian covered asset, responsible entity or statutory application is established. Its provider-to-provider notification and independent investigation are evaluated as evidenced governance responses, not Australian statutory compliance. Forty represented voluntary assessments cannot establish that no binding duties existed elsewhere.

Broader enforceable AI incident reporting would require explicit policy choices: governed actors and territorial nexus; AI incident/harm definitions; actual and imminent trigger thresholds; knowledge attribution and clocks; recipients and secure channels; initial and follow-up content; confidentiality, individual protection and lawful information sharing; investigative powers and independence; enforcement and review; coordination with overlapping privacy, cyber and sectoral duties. These are proposed design questions, not enacted SOCI obligations. Any broader policy should explain what gap remains after existing conditional duties rather than assume that every AI incident is already covered or wholly unregulated.
